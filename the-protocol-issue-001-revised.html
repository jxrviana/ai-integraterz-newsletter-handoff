REGULATORY
FDA Removes Cardiovascular Boxed Warning From All Testosterone Products
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. Wide shot inside a modern men's health clinic exam room, morning light through frosted glass.
THE SHORT VERSION
On February 28, 2025, the FDA informed sponsors of all testosterone products about new class-wide labeling changes. The headline move: removal of the Boxed Warning about increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The change followed the TRAVERSE clinical trial, which concluded that testosterone therapy was not associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiac events among men with hypogonadism and elevated cardiovascular risk. The FDA retained limitation-of-use language for age-related hypogonadism while also adding new blood pressure warnings based on ambulatory blood pressure monitoring studies.
THE DETAILS
• The TRAVERSE trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2023, was the industry-wide safety study the FDA requested in 2014 after increased reports of stroke, heart attack, and death in men taking testosterone products.
• The TRAVERSE results, combined with completed ambulatory blood pressure monitoring studies, gave the FDA a complete evidence base to act on: removing the cardiovascular warning while adding product-specific blood pressure information.
• Current FDA-approved testosterone formulations include oral, topical gel, transdermal patch, buccal system, and injection. All are affected by the class-wide labeling changes.
• The limitation-of-use language for age-related hypogonadism was retained. The FDA did not open the door for prescribing testosterone solely for aging; it removed a safety warning that had been contradicted by subsequent evidence.
WHY IT MATTERS
This is the single biggest regulatory shift in testosterone therapy in over a decade. For clinic operators, the removal of the cardiovascular boxed warning reduces a major source of liability anxiety for prescribing physicians. Medical directors who have been uneasy about the tension between standard of care and approved labeling now have a regulatory foundation that matches the clinical evidence.

CLINICAL
The TRAVERSE Trial Changed the Safety Conversation. Here Is What Clinic Operators Should Know.
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. Macro photograph of a blood draw station, a single vial being labeled by a gloved hand.
THE SHORT VERSION
The TRAVERSE trial (Testosterone Replacement Therapy for Assessment of Long-term Vascular Events and Efficacy Response in Hypogonadal Men), published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2023, was the definitive safety study the FDA requested after a 2014 advisory committee meeting flagged increased reports of cardiovascular events. The trial's conclusion: that testosterone therapy was not associated with increased risk of major adverse cardiac events, directly led to the FDA's February 2025 removal of the cardiovascular boxed warning. The downstream effects are now reshaping clinical practice across men's health clinics and primary care settings.
THE DETAILS
• The TRAVERSE trial enrolled over 5,200 men with hypogonadism and preexisting cardiovascular disease or elevated risk, randomizing them to testosterone gel or placebo. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiac events.
• The trial found no statistically significant difference in cardiovascular events between the testosterone and placebo groups, a result that contradicted the safety concerns that drove the 2015 FDA communication.
• With the boxed warning removed, primary care physicians face fewer liability concerns when prescribing testosterone, which may accelerate the shift of initial TRT management from specialty clinics to PCP offices.
• Men's health clinic operators should position themselves as the destination for advanced protocols that PCPs do not want to manage. The regulatory shift expands the funnel at the top and creates a natural referral gradient.
WHY IT MATTERS
When the safety narrative shifts at the FDA level, it changes prescribing behavior at scale. The removal of the boxed warning is not just a regulatory footnote. It is a signal to every primary care physician, insurer, and health system that the evidence base for testosterone therapy has strengthened. Clinic operators who understand the TRAVERSE data can use it in their referral conversations with PCPs who may still be operating under the old safety assumptions.

CLINIC GROWTH
Everyone Wants a Referral Channel. Almost Nobody Builds the Part That Makes It Real.
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. One man in workout clothes handing his phone to another in a clinic waiting area.
THE SHORT VERSION
Most clinics treat referrals as a passive hope. They ask patients to tell a friend, maybe print a card, and then wonder why the channel never produces. The reality is that referral volume is an operationalization problem: an engineering challenge, not a marketing one. With the affiliate marketing industry projected to reach $13.81 billion in spend in 2026, clinics that treat their referral channels with the same structural rigor as a formal referral program will capture disproportionate share.
THE DETAILS
• The Power of Eight: eight well-engineered referral sources, each with a defined trigger moment and zero-friction handoff, can sustain a clinic's entire new-patient pipeline without paid advertising.
• Tracking is the difference between a channel and a hope. Every new patient must be tagged to a specific referral source. Without source attribution, you cannot optimize what you cannot measure.
• Terms matter. Both the referrer and the referred patient need to understand exactly what is being offered. Clear, written terms eliminate the ambiguity that kills conversion.
• Follow-through closes the loop. A monthly referral roundup email tells referrers what happened with the patients they sent. Without this feedback loop, referrers stop referring.

PRESCRIBING
Testosterone Prescribing Is Rising Again After a Decade-Long Slide
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. Overhead shot of a clinic director's desk at golden hour: leather notebook, fountain pen, printed lab panel.
THE SHORT VERSION
Epic Research published data on July 29, 2026, showing that the share of male patients prescribed testosterone fell from 0.83% in 2013 to 0.64% by 2021, then rose to 0.93% in 2025 and reached 0.95% in the partial first half of 2026. Every age group 30 and older surpassed its early-2010s peak by 2025. Men aged 50 to 64 had the highest prevalence at 1.57% in 2025. The study examined 109 million men across 2,000 hospitals and 49,000 clinics in all 50 states.
THE DETAILS
• Testosterone prescribing prevalence bottomed out at 0.64% of male patients in 2021, then began climbing around 2022. The recovery accelerated through the first half of 2026, reaching 0.95%, surpassing the 2013 peak of 0.83%.
• Men aged 50 to 64 maintained the highest prevalence throughout the study period, reaching 1.57% in 2025, up from a prior peak of 1.30% in 2013. Men aged 18 to 29 had the lowest rates, ranging between 0.10% and 0.15% throughout.
• The 2015 FDA safety communication triggered the initial decline; the February 2025 removal of the boxed warning and growth of telehealth men's health services contributed to the recovery.
• The data comes from Cosmos, a dataset representing more than 310 million patient records. As a descriptive analysis, the study does not attribute the trend to any single cause, but the timeline aligns with major regulatory and market changes.
WHY IT MATTERS
The addressable market for clinic services is expanding, not contracting. The prescribing rebound signals that both physician confidence and patient demand have recovered past the pre-2015 baseline. Primary care physicians are increasingly comfortable initiating testosterone therapy, creating a referral dynamic where men's health clinic operators can position themselves as the destination for advanced protocols.

THE PLAYBOOK
ONE USEFUL MOVE
The Lab Panel Audit
This is a one-hour exercise you can do this week that will surface hidden revenue in your existing patient base.
Step 1: Pull a report of every active patient who has not had labs drawn in the past 120 days.
Step 2: Cross-reference against your scheduling system. Flag every patient with no upcoming appointment.
Step 3: Have a medical assistant send one standardized message: "Your clinician would like to review your levels before your next protocol adjustment. Can we get you in for labs this week? We have a standing order ready."
Step 4: Track how many of those patients book within 72 hours of receiving the message. If even 30 percent rebook, downstream protocol adjustment appointments multiply that number.
