FDA Asks Drugmakers to Strip Age-Based Restrictions From Testosterone Labels
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. Wide shot inside a modern men's health clinic exam room, morning light through frosted glass.
THE SHORT VERSION
The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services formally requested that manufacturers of FDA-approved testosterone products update their labeling. The key change: remove language restricting use to men with 'classical hypogonadism' caused by specific medical conditions like pituitary tumors or genetic disorders. The HHS request opens the door for a broader clinical definition of who qualifies for testosterone therapy, aligning labels with what many practicing clinicians have been doing for years.
THE DETAILS
• The HHS request, confirmed by multiple outlets in late July 2026, targets age-based and condition-based restrictions in current testosterone product labels.
• If manufacturers comply, the labeling change removes a major liability concern for prescribing physicians at men's health clinics.
• The current label creates a tension between standard of care and approved labeling that has made medical directors uneasy for years.
• Testosterone prescriptions peaked at roughly 7.4 million annually before the FDA's 2015 safety communication triggered a multi-year decline; prescribing has now begun rising again.
WHY IT MATTERS
This is the single biggest regulatory shift in testosterone therapy in over a decade. For clinic operators, it means fewer barriers to protocol design and more defensible prescribing documentation. It may also accelerate insurance coverage expansion, though that piece remains uncertain.

The Pentagon Now Wants to Test Every Service Member's Testosterone
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. Macro photograph of a blood draw station, a single vial being labeled by a gloved hand.
THE SHORT VERSION
The U.S. Department of Defense announced plans to implement widespread testosterone screening across all active-duty service members. The stated rationale is that testosterone levels affect readiness, recovery, and cognitive performance under stress. The policy would represent the largest institutional testosterone screening initiative ever undertaken in the United States, potentially involving over 1.3 million active-duty personnel.
THE DETAILS
• The DoD screening initiative, reported by CNBC and Reuters in July 2026, would cover the entire active-duty force.
• The military treats testosterone as a biomarker worth monitoring across the entire force, normalizing screening at an institutional scale.
• A 2024 study in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism found that roughly 20 percent of men over 40 have testosterone levels below clinical threshold.
• If screening identifies low levels in even a modest fraction of service members, the referral pipeline to civilian TRT clinics grows substantially.
WHY IT MATTERS
Normalization at scale: when the U.S. military treats testosterone as a biomarker worth monitoring across its entire force, it sends a powerful signal to every employer, insurer, and primary care practice in the country.

Three structural failures kill referral channels at clinics: no defined trigger moment, no zero-friction handoff, and no feedback loop. None of it requires custom software.

PRESCRIBING
Testosterone Prescribing Is Rising Again After a Decade-Long Slide
Image source: original photography for The Protocol. Overhead shot of a clinic director's desk at golden hour: leather notebook, fountain pen, printed lab panel.
THE SHORT VERSION
Epic Research published data on July 29, 2026, showing that testosterone prescribing rates in the United States have begun climbing again after nearly ten years of decline. The initial drop followed an FDA safety communication in 2015 that warned of possible cardiovascular risks associated with testosterone therapy, a warning that subsequent research has substantially complicated and in some cases contradicted.
THE DETAILS
• Epic Research data confirms prescribing rates reached their lowest point around 2020-2021, roughly 40 percent below the 2014 peak.
• The recovery accelerated through the first half of 2026 as the safety narrative shifted and prescribing behavior changed at scale.
• Primary care physicians are increasingly comfortable initiating or continuing testosterone therapy as new research complicates the 2015 safety warning.
• The addressable market for clinic services is expanding, not contracting, as prescribing rebounds.
WHY IT MATTERS
The prescribing rebound signals that primary care physicians are increasingly comfortable with testosterone therapy, creating a referral dynamic where specialists and clinic operators can position themselves as the destination for advanced protocols that PCPs do not want to manage.

THE PLAYBOOK
ONE USEFUL MOVE
The Lab Panel Audit
Step 1: Pull a report of every active patient who has not had labs drawn in the past 120 days.
Step 2: Cross-reference against your scheduling system. Flag every patient with no upcoming appointment.
Step 3: Have a medical assistant send one standardized message: 'Dr. [Name] would like to review your levels before your next protocol adjustment. Can we get you in for labs this week? We have a standing order ready.'
Step 4: Track how many of those patients book within 72 hours of receiving the message. If even 30 percent rebook, downstream protocol adjustment appointments multiply that number.
