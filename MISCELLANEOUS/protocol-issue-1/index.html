FDA
🧪 FDA Panel Weighs Seven Peptides Thursday

The Rundown: FDA’s Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee meets July 23–24 at the agency’s White Oak campus to consider whether seven peptides belong on the 503A bulks list. The meeting follows FDA’s April 15 announcement that a group of peptides would leave Category 2 within seven calendar days after their nominations were withdrawn, and an April 16 Federal Register notice scheduling the session.

The details:

  • July 23 agenda: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, and MOTs-C. July 24: Emideltide/DSIP, Semax, and Epitalon.
  • Five more are queued for a second PCAC meeting before the end of February 2027 — GHK-Cu, Melanotan II, Cathelicidin (LL-37), Dihexa acetate, and pegylated Mechano Growth Factor.
  • Category 2 means significant safety risks and ineligibility for FDA’s interim enforcement-discretion policy. FDA moved these peptides there in September 2023, citing immunogenicity risk and limited human clinical data.
  • The correction a lot of operators are getting wrong: removal from Category 2 does not authorize compounding. Per the source, “Removal alone should not be understood as a new FDA determination of eligibility. These substances have not been formally added to the 503A bulks list.”

Why it matters: A PCAC recommendation shapes what a compounding partner will legally make six to twelve months from now. Any inventory order, protocol revision, or landing page written this week is being written against a rule that may move on Thursday — and anyone treating April’s Category 2 removal as a green light is already exposed.

DEA
📅 Your Telehealth Prescribing Rules Expire In December

The Rundown: DEA and HHS issued a fourth temporary extension of COVID-era telemedicine prescribing flexibilities, running January 1 through December 31, 2026. Practitioners may keep prescribing Schedule II–V controlled substances by telemedicine without an initial in-person examination. Testosterone and its esters are Schedule III, so this rule governs telehealth TRT prescribing directly.

The details:

  • The extension imposed no new documentation, registration, or technology requirements, and applies to all practitioner-patient relationships regardless of when the relationship began.
  • The condition is unchanged: the prescription must be “issued for a legitimate medical purpose and complies with all other federal and state requirements.”
  • DEA says it intends “to issue final regulations before the new 2026 deadline” and is still reviewing extensive public comments on the proposed permanent rules. Both law-firm analyses describe the extension as a bridge past a “telemedicine cliff.”
  • DEA’s January 2025 proposed Special Registration framework contemplates three tiers, one of which is a Telemedicine Platform Registration required for platforms that connect patients to prescribers — company-level, not just prescriber-level. That is proposed-rule content, not current law.

Why it matters: Every remote-prescribing men’s health business is operating on a permission slip with roughly five months left on it, and the replacement is not finished. If the platform-registration tier survives into the final rule, the compliance burden moves from your prescribers to your company. That is a budget line and a legal review, and both belong on the calendar now rather than in November.

📈 Hims Adds Testosterone To A $3B Run

The Rundown: Hims & Hers reported Q1 2026 revenue of $608 million on nearly 2.6 million subscribers and raised full-year guidance to $2.8–$3.0 billion. On the earnings call the company named testosterone among its expansion categories, alongside weight loss, labs, and menopause — putting the largest DTC player in the space into the men’s hormone market.

The details:

  • Q1 2026 revenue: $608 million, up 4% year over year. Subscribers: nearly 2.6 million, up 9% year over year.
  • Full-year 2026 guidance: $2.8–$3.0 billion revenue, 19–28% growth, with adjusted EBITDA of $275–$350 million.
  • Gross margin 65% GAAP and 70% adjusted. Cash and short-term investments $751 million. GAAP net loss for the quarter was $92 million.
  • International revenue grew nearly tenfold to $78 million. No standalone testosterone revenue figure was disclosed in this source.

Why it matters: A company holding $751 million in cash with 2.6 million subscribers already on the books can cross-sell hormone services to customers it has already paid to acquire, at close to zero incremental acquisition cost. No independent clinic outspends that. What is left to compete on are the things a national subscription structurally cannot offer: in-person labs, a named physician, and a local referral network.

RETENTION
📊 GLP-1 Persistence Nearly Doubled In Three Years

The Rundown: A study of 33,607 commercially insured adults without diabetes found one-year persistence on high-potency weight-loss GLP-1s rose from 33.2% in 2021 to 60.9% in the first half of 2024. Adherence moved on the same curve. The authors credit resolved supply shortages, better dose-escalation protocols, side-effect management, and lifestyle-program adoption.

The details:

  • One-year persistence by year: 33.2% in 2021, 34.1% in 2022, 40.2% in 2023, 60.9% in the first half of 2024.
  • By drug in the first half of 2024: semaglutide 58.6%, tirzepatide 64.8%. Tirzepatide was 64.0% in 2023.
  • Mean proportion of days covered rose from 52.6% in 2021 to 72.8% in the first half of 2024. The share of patients reaching 80% adherence or better went from 30.2% to 55.5%.
  • Persistence was defined as no gap longer than 60 days between the end of a days-supply and the next fill, within 365 days of starting.

Why it matters: Three of the four causes the authors name — dose escalation, side-effect management, structured lifestyle support — are protocol decisions a clinic controls, not properties of the drug. That moves twelve-month retention out of the patient-motivation column and into the operations column, and it hands operators a published number to measure their own cohort against. Real-world persistence still trails the above-80% figures seen in trials.

CASE STUDY
💳 Hone Health: $113M ARR On $129 Memberships

The Rundown: Hone Health, a telehealth hormone-optimization platform, grew from $69.7 million ARR at the end of 2024 to $113.53 million by September 2025 — 82% year over year. The model is a $129–$149 monthly membership with medication add-ons priced separately. Average customer spend runs $225 a month.

The details:

  • Add-on pricing: testosterone injection $28 a month, anastrozole $22 a month. Annual testosterone treatment at Hone works out to roughly $1,884.
  • Volume to date: 300,000 patients tested, 55,000 patients treated. Monthly growth accelerated from about 3% to 6% during 2025.
  • 65% of customers earn between $50,000 and $100,000 a year.
  • $39 million raised in total, including a $33 million Series A in January 2025 led by Tribe Capital. Alongside the round, Hone acquired ivee, an in-home phlebotomy provider, adding blood draw and IV therapy.

Why it matters: The gap between the $129 headline price and the $225 average spend is the whole model. The membership buys the relationship; the add-ons carry the margin. And the income mix — most customers between $50K and $100K — retires the premise that hormone optimization is a luxury-priced offer. It is a mass-market subscription, and it is priced like one.

THE PLAYBOOK
 
The Three Channels That Work When You Can’t Run Ads

Platform policy takes large parts of paid acquisition off the table for this industry. What is left is not a consolation prize — it is three channels that compound, and they are all worked the same way: help first, ask second.

1. Power partners. These are the businesses that already have your customer at the moment he decides something has to change — primary care physicians, urologists, chiropractors, physical therapists, dietitians and nutritionists, gyms and personal trainers, med spas, and the HR or benefits lead at any large local employer. It works because the referral arrives pre-trusted; he is not evaluating you, he is taking his physical therapist’s word for it. First move: open with what you can do for them — a men’s-health talk for their members, a co-branded offer their clients get, or a referral of your patients to them. Never open with “will you refer to us.”

2. Niche influencers. Fitness, longevity, and biohacking creators, plus the local athletic-community figures — gym owners, coaches, former athletes — who hold a room without holding a large following. Run the fit test before you spend anything: is the audience local or in-category, does it skew male 30–65, and does the creator already talk about health and self-improvement so that a men’s-health mention is not a swerve? Three yes answers, pursue. First move: propose a collaboration that helps their audience rather than a paid post that helps you — a Q&A, a “what my bloodwork actually said” episode, a perk their community gets. Give every creator a unique code or landing page so you know who actually produces.

3. Podcast placements. Health-optimization and longevity shows for the clinical lead, local business shows for the owner. A men’s health business sells trust before it sells anything else, and 30 to 45 minutes of the owner or medical lead teaching reaches the man searching “low energy” at 11pm better than any ad slot would. First move: pitch the host on what their listeners get, not on who you are. Name the segment, offer three angles and a one-sheet, and close every appearance with one soft call to action and a trackable link.
