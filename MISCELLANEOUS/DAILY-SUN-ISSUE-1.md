SALES

💸 **[Your Cost To Win A Customer Just Jumped 40%](https://www.woodmac.com/news/opinion/us-residential-solar-customer-acquisition-costs-set-to-spike-40-in-2026-before-gradual-decline/)**

[IMAGE — s1: A homeowner's front door photographed at eye level in hard late-afternoon side light, a single unread flyer wedged in the frame, the suburban street behind it thrown out of focus.]

**The Short Version:** Wood Mackenzie forecasts that US residential solar customer acquisition cost will rise from $0.60 per watt in 2025 — a five-year low — to $0.84 per watt in 2026. That is a 40% increase, and it lands in the same year the 30% federal credit disappeared and the residential market is forecast to contract by roughly a fifth.

**The Details:**

- Customer acquisition cost: $0.60/W in 2025, a five-year low, forecast at $0.84/W for 2026 — a 40% year-over-year increase.
- Three drivers named: the 30% Section 25D credit expired in December 2025, removing the incentive behind the 2025 demand surge; a shrinking addressable market leaves more installers chasing fewer qualified buyers; and companies are absorbing transition costs as they build customer-lifetime-value models around storage, EV chargers and referral systems.
- Hardware moved the other way. Wood Mackenzie's separate June 10 market release put Q1 2026 module prices at $0.34/Wdc, down 21% year over year, with residential system pricing at $3.21/Wdc, down 7%.
- The piece describes installers shifting away from outsourced dealer sales toward in-house acquisition, and states the reason directly: *"Without the 30% ITC buffer, some installers can no longer afford to pass on the costs of high dealer fees."*

**Why It Matters:** This is the number that reprices every solar business this year. A 40% increase in acquisition cost, against hardware that got cheaper and a ticket size that didn't grow, comes straight out of margin — and it is the one line on your P&L that no supplier negotiation touches. Whatever your cost per sale was in 2025, price 2026 against $0.84.

POLICY

⏳ **[The Safe Harbor Window Closed Seventeen Days Ago](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2026/feb/navigating-safe-harbor-rules-for-solar-and-wind-sec-48e-facilities/)**

[IMAGE — s2: A single shrink-wrapped pallet of solar modules alone in a half-empty warehouse bay, hard shafts of afternoon sun coming through a raised roll-up door with dust visible in the beams.]

**The Short Version:** Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, wind and solar facilities that begin construction after July 4, 2026 must be placed in service by December 31, 2027 to qualify for the Section 48E credit. Projects that began construction between January 1 and July 4 of this year have until December 31, 2030. That window closed seventeen days ago.

**The Details:**

- Three tiers by construction start date: began before December 31, 2025 → in service by December 31, 2029. Began January 1 through July 4, 2026 → December 31, 2030. Began after July 4, 2026 → December 31, 2027.
- Notice 2025-42, issued August 15, 2025 and applying to construction beginning on or after September 2, 2025, limits solar and wind to the physical-work test — with an exception for low-output solar at 1.5 MW or less, which keeps both options.
- The 5% cost test under Notice 2022-61 treats construction as begun when a taxpayer pays or incurs at least 5% of total facility cost and makes continuous efforts to complete the facility. The physical-work test has no fixed dollar or percentage threshold and is judged on the nature of the work.
- Prohibited Foreign Entity material-assistance thresholds require 50% non-PFE sourcing in 2026, rising to 55% in 2027 and after. Executive Order 14315, signed July 7, 2025, directed guidance to prevent circumvention of the credit's termination.

**Why It Matters:** Two installers bidding the same street are now on different clocks. Whoever safe-harbored or started physical work before July 4 has a runway to 2030; whoever didn't is racing an 18-month deadline with permitting and interconnection eating most of it. Residential systems sit well under the 1.5 MW line, so the 5% cost test is still available on that side — which is exactly the detail worth confirming with your CPA this week rather than in Q4.

📉 **[Nearly Half Of Installers Lost Volume Last Year](https://aurorasolar.com/resources/2026-aurora-solar-snapshot/)**

[IMAGE — s5: A near-empty installation yard at midday photographed from the far corner, two remaining racking bundles and one idle boom lift on a wide expanse of concrete under flat grey light.]

**The Short Version:** Aurora Solar's 2026 Snapshot — vendor survey research from an installer-software company, based on 1,112 homeowners, 600-plus solar professionals and analysis of 20 million-plus projects — found 46% of installers reported a decline in 2025 sales volume and 54% said competition intensified. Homeowner demand has not vanished: 71% still express at least some interest.

**The Details:**

- 46% of installers reported lower 2025 sales volume, 54% said competition intensified, and 63% believe OBBB passage will negatively affect their business.
- 22% of homeowners said they worry their installer will cease operations, and 41% named company trustworthiness as a concern. 67% prioritize strong warranties and support.
- Installers named reputation and reviews (49%) and customer experience (48%) as how they win, ahead of price (30%). Among homeowners, 82% said monthly payment amount matters most.
- Median residential system: 7.92 kW, down 6.49% year over year, at a national median price of $3.44/W, down 14.21%. Diversification is already widespread — 71% of installers offer EV charging and 65% offer roofing.

**Why It Matters:** Put two of Aurora's numbers next to each other — 22% of homeowners now worry their installer will fold, and 49% of installers say reputation is how they win — and the conclusion is that trust has passed price as the primary conversion lever. The bankruptcy wave isn't only removing competitors; it is taxing the survivors' close rates. Every review, warranty page and finished job is now doing acquisition work.

**The Details:**

**Why It Matters:** This is the cleanest available evidence that the volume business and the value business have separated. Sunrun installed a fifth less solar and booked 43% more revenue by monetizing a subscriber base it already owned. The honest counterweight belongs in the same breath: cost per addition rose 18% and quarterly cash generation went negative. It is a real pivot under real strain, not a victory lap.

### Where Solar Leads Come From When Ads Stop Working

[IMAGE — s7: Three pairs of tradesmen's work boots lined up on a homeowner's front porch step at golden hour, the front door standing ajar behind them, shot low and tight.]

Paid lead channels reprice against you the moment demand tightens, and per Wood Mackenzie they just did. What's left is three channels that compound instead of inflating — and all three are worked the same way: help first, ask second.

**1. Power partners.** These are the trades and professionals already inside the house at the moment a homeowner has decided to spend money on it — roofers, electricians, HVAC contractors, realtors, mortgage brokers, home inspectors, property managers, and EV-charger installers. It works because the referral arrives pre-trusted and pre-timed: the homeowner isn't evaluating you, he's taking his roofer's word for it, in the same week he already accepted that the house needs work. **First move:** open with what you can do for them — send them the roof jobs your assessments turn up, offer their clients a no-charge shade and production analysis, or co-quote a reroof-and-solar package where they own the roof scope. Never open with "will you refer to us."

**2. Niche creators.** Local home-improvement and home-energy creators, regional lifestyle accounts with a real geographic center of gravity, and the YouTube energy-nerd channels whose audiences already understand a kilowatt-hour. Run the fit test before you spend anything: is the audience actually in your service territory or genuinely in-category, does it skew homeowner rather than renter, and does the creator already talk about bills, houses and equipment so a solar segment isn't a swerve? Three yes answers, pursue. **First move:** propose a collaboration that helps their audience instead of a paid post that helps you — a teardown of a real utility bill, an honest "what solar didn't do for me" episode, a walkthrough of one install start to finish. Give every creator a unique code or landing page so you know who actually produces.

**3. Podcast placements.** Local business shows for the owner, home-improvement and contractor shows for the operations lead, regional real-estate podcasts for anyone who can talk resale and appraisal. Thirty to forty-five minutes of a named human explaining how the economics actually work reaches a homeowner deciding on a $30,000 purchase in a way no ad slot does — and it's the same trust problem Aurora's survey says is now deciding deals. **First move:** pitch the host on what their listeners get, not on who you are. Name the segment, offer three angles and a one-sheet, then close every appearance with a single soft call to action and a trackable link.
