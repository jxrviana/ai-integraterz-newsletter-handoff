## 1. FDA Panel Weighs Seven Peptides Thursday

**Category:** FDA / PEPTIDES
**Source:** The National Law Review — "Tiny Chains, Big Changes? What FDA's Latest Actions Mean for Peptide Compounding"
**URL:** https://natlawreview.com/article/tiny-chains-big-changes-what-fdas-latest-actions-mean-peptide-compounding
**Corroborating source:** FDA Law Blog (Hyman, Phelps & McNamara) — https://www.thefdalawblog.com/2026/04/fdas-peptide-rally-what-compounders-and-industry-need-to-know-post-1-of-2/
**Date:** FDA actions April 15–16, 2026; PCAC meeting July 23–24, 2026

**The Rundown:** On April 15, 2026, FDA announced that a group of peptides would be removed from 503A Category 2 within seven calendar days because the underlying nominations were withdrawn. The next day FDA published a Federal Register notice scheduling a Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) meeting for July 23–24, 2026, to consider whether these peptides belong on the 503A bulks list. Category 2 designation had effectively barred 503A pharmacies from compounding them since September 2023.

**The details:**
- Seven peptides are on the July 23–24 agenda: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, and MOTs-C (July 23); Emideltide/DSIP, Semax, and Epitalon (July 24).
- Five more are queued for a second PCAC meeting before the end of February 2027: GHK-Cu, Melanotan II, Cathelicidin (LL-37), Dihexa acetate, and Pegylated Mechano Growth Factor (PEG-MGF).
- Category definitions: Category 1 = may qualify for FDA's interim enforcement-discretion policy; Category 2 = significant safety risks, ineligible for enforcement discretion; Category 3 = insufficient nomination information.
- FDA moved these peptides into Category 2 in September 2023, citing safety concerns including immunogenicity risk and limited human clinical data (per FDA Law Blog).
- Critical caveat, stated explicitly by the source: removal from Category 2 does **not** authorize compounding. "Removal alone should not be understood as a new FDA determination of eligibility. These substances have not been formally added to the 503A bulks list."
- The meeting is at FDA's White Oak Campus, Silver Spring, MD.

**Why it matters:** For any clinic or brand whose peptide line sits in regulatory limbo, this is the week the limbo either starts resolving or hardens. A PCAC recommendation shapes what a compounding partner will legally make six to twelve months out — which means inventory, protocol, and marketing decisions made now are being made against a rule that could move on Thursday.

**Pillar:** 1 — Regulatory weather.

## 2. FDA Moves To Close The GLP-1 Compounding Door

**Category:** FDA / GLP-1
**Source:** Orrick — "FDA Moves to Shut the Door on Large-Scale Compounding of GLP-1 Drugs"
**URL:** https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/05/FDA-Moves-to-Shut-the-Door-on-Large-Scale-Compounding-of-GLP1-Drugs
**Date:** FDA proposal April 30, 2026; article May 2026

**The Rundown:** On April 30, 2026, FDA proposed excluding semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from the 503B Bulks List — the list identifying bulk drug substances outsourcing facilities may legally compound from. FDA's stated basis is that there is no clinical need for outsourcing facilities to compound these drugs from bulk API. The public comment window closed June 29, 2026.

**The details:**
- The three drugs named: semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide.
- The 503B Bulks List "identifies bulk drug substances that outsourcing facilities may use in compounding under section 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act."
- 503B outsourcing facilities take the most severe hit — without bulks-list authorization or a shortage designation, they cannot compound these at scale.
- 503A pharmacy compounders are not directly changed by this proposal but remain constrained by existing prohibitions on copying commercially available drugs.
- Telehealth firms relying on bulk compounding "could significantly impact" operations if the rule is finalized.
- Comment deadline: June 29, 2026, via the Federal Register docket.

**Why it matters:** If finalized, the compounded-GLP-1 supply chain that a large share of men's health and weight-loss programs were built on stops being a legal supply chain. Operators still running compounded GLP-1 as a core offer are running a revenue line with a visible expiration date, and the pivot options — branded partnerships or exiting the category — take months to stand up.

**Pillar:** 1 — Regulatory weather.

## 3. Your Telehealth Prescribing Rules Expire In December

**Category:** DEA / TELEHEALTH
**Source:** Holland & Knight — "DEA and HHS Extend Telemedicine Prescribing Flexibilities Through 2026"
**URL:** https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/01/dea-and-hhs-extend-telemedicine-prescribing-flexibilities-through-2026
**Corroborating source:** Frier Levitt — https://www.frierlevitt.com/articles/dea-hhs-fourth-extension-telemedicine-controlled-substances-2026/
**Date:** Rule published in Federal Register December 31, 2025; H&K analysis January 13, 2026

**The Rundown:** DEA and HHS issued a fourth temporary extension of COVID-era telemedicine prescribing flexibilities, running January 1 through December 31, 2026. Practitioners may continue prescribing Schedule II–V controlled substances via telemedicine without an initial in-person examination. Testosterone and its esters are Schedule III, so this rule governs telehealth TRT prescribing directly.

**The details:**
- Effective window: January 1, 2026 through December 31, 2026. Formal rule scheduled for Federal Register publication December 31, 2025.
- Covers Schedule II–V controlled substances.
- Condition: the prescription must be "issued for a legitimate medical purpose and complies with all other federal and state requirements."
- No new documentation, registration, or technology requirements were imposed by the extension.
- Applies uniformly to all practitioner-patient relationships regardless of when the relationship was established.
- DEA states it intends "to issue final regulations before the new 2026 deadline" and is "still reviewing extensive public comments on the proposed permanent rules." Both sources describe the extension as a bridge to avoid a "telemedicine cliff."
- Separately, DEA's January 2025 proposed Special Registration framework contemplates three tiers: Telemedicine Prescribing Registration (Schedule III–V), Advanced Telemedicine Prescribing Registration (Schedule II–V, restricted to specified board-certified specialties), and a Telemedicine Platform Registration required for platforms that connect patients to prescribers. Source: Fenwick — https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/understanding-the-deas-new-telemedicine-rules-faq-for-healthcare-providers-and-telehealth-companies (note: this Fenwick piece predates the fourth extension and states a since-superseded December 31, 2025 expiration; the tier structure is the proposed-rule content, not current law).

**Why it matters:** Every telehealth TRT business is operating on a permission slip that expires in roughly five months, and the replacement rule — which may require platform-level DEA registration, not just prescriber-level — is not final. This is the single largest structural risk on the calendar for remote-prescribing men's health businesses.

## 4. FDA Sent 30 Telehealth Firms Warning Letters

**Category:** FDA / ADVERTISING
**Source:** Foley & Lardner — "GLP-1 Compliance: FDA Targets Telehealth Marketing in 30 New Warning Letters"
**URL:** https://www.foley.com/p/102mmr0/glp1-compliance-fda-targets-telehealth-marketing-in-30-new-warning-letters/
**Corroborating source:** EMJ — https://www.emjreviews.com/diabetes/news/fda-warns-30-telehealth-companies-against-illegal-glp-1-marketing/
**Date:** Warning letters issued/announced early March 2026 (Foley states March 3, 2026; EMJ published March 7, 2026 referring to an announcement "on Tuesday")

**The Rundown:** FDA issued 30 warning letters to telehealth companies over how they marketed compounded GLP-1 products. The letters targeted website marketing language, not the compounding itself — specifically claims implying the compounded products were equivalent to, or approved like, FDA-approved drugs, and branding that obscured who actually compounded the product.

**The details:**
- Three violation categories cited: (1) placing the telehealth firm's own brand name on labels "in a way that suggests they compound the drugs when they do not"; (2) marketing practices failing to qualify for 503A or 503B exemptions; (3) promotional materials making "new drug claims and imply that FDA has approved, or otherwise evaluated, the compounded products for safety and effectiveness."
- Specific offending phrases quoted by FDA include "Generic Zepbound," "Contains the active pharmaceutical ingredient in Wegovy," and "This FDA-approved treatment promotes safe, effective weight loss."
- FDA warned that failure to correct "may result in enforcement action, including seizure or injunction without further notice." For foreign companies, FDA may detain products at the border.
- FDA Commissioner Marty Makary: "It's a new era. We are paying close attention to misleading claims being made by telehealth and pharma companies."
- Foley's compliance guidance: avoid implying "equivalence, interchangeability with, or approval in the same manner as FDA-approved products," and scrub promotional materials for unsubstantiated weight-loss claims.

**Why it matters:** This is a marketing-copy enforcement action, not a clinical one — meaning the exposure sits with whoever writes the website, the funnel, and the ad. Operators who describe compounded products by reference to brand-name drugs are using the exact phrasing FDA quoted back at 30 companies.

**Pillar:** 1 — Regulatory weather (with direct read-through to pillar 4, acquisition).

## 5. Med Spas Kept Selling After The Shortage Ended

**Category:** COMPLIANCE / MARKET
**Source:** Healio — "Compounded semaglutide, tirzepatide continued to be sold after shortages ended"
**URL:** https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20260717/compounded-semaglutide-tirzepatide-continued-to-be-sold-after-shortages-ended
**Underlying study:** JAMA Health Forum, 2026
**Date:** Published July 17, 2026; fieldwork August–October 2025

**The Rundown:** A cross-sectional secret-shopper study of brick-and-mortar weight-loss clinics and medical spas in Oklahoma and West Virginia found that the overwhelming majority were still selling compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide after the FDA shortage designations ended. The study also documented additives, staffing models, and gaps in the sterile-compounding credentials of supplying pharmacies.

**The details:**
- 75 businesses confirmed selling compounded medications; 92.0% offered compounded semaglutide, 86.7% offered compounded tirzepatide; one sold an unnamed triple receptor agonist.
- 58.7% sold compounded medications with additives; vitamin B was the most common.
- Of 23 supplying pharmacies: 95.6% held active state licenses, 13% were FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facilities, and 19% of state-licensed facilities lacked sterile compounding authorization.
- One supplying facility had received two FDA warning letters for sterile compounding violations; two faced state sanctions for the same.
- Staffing: nurse practitioners or physician assistants at 56% of businesses; MDs/DOs at 40%; RNs at 1.3%.
- Operations: 69.3% required in-person visits; 61.3% mandated monthly follow-ups.
- Researcher DiStefano's guidance: "You shouldn't purchase compounded GLP-1 products from vendors unwilling to disclose the supplying pharmacy name."

**Why it matters:** This is the map of what regulators and plaintiffs' lawyers will read next, and it names the weak link — the supplying pharmacy, where nearly one in five state-licensed facilities lacked sterile compounding authorization. Any operator who cannot name their compounder and produce its credentials is carrying someone else's risk.

**Pillar:** 1 and 3 — Regulatory weather / clinical-product developments.

## 6. Online GLP-1 Sellers Skipped The Screening

**Category:** TELEHEALTH / RISK
**Source:** Congress.net — "Online GLP-1 Prescriptions Approved In Minutes, Raising Serious Safety Concerns"
**URL:** https://congress.net/online-glp-1-prescriptions-approved-in-minutes-raising-serious-safety-concerns/
**Corroborating source:** STAT — https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/06/glp-1-telehealth-prescriptions-jama-yale-secret-shopper-study/ (loads, partially paywalled; confirms Yale researcher, JAMA, published July 6, 2026)
**Underlying study:** JAMA, published July 6, 2026; fieldwork August–December 2025

**The Rundown:** A Yale-led secret-shopper study posed as patients across 49 websites selling branded or compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide. Nearly all issued a prescription, most within a day, and a minority involved any live clinician contact at all. The study is now the most-cited public evidence on how thin telehealth screening actually is.

**The details:**
- 49 websites examined; 45 (91.8%) issued a prescription; 34 actually mailed the drug.
- Median time to prescription: one day or less. Two compounded orders were approved in under five minutes.
- Only 27 sites (55%) asked about eating disorder history.
- Only 26 sites (53%) asked about diet and physical activity.
- Only 13 sites (27%) required a video consultation with a clinician; only 3 required a phone call before prescribing.
- Researchers' conclusion: "Limited clinician engagement, especially when prescribing compounded GLP-1 RAs with uncertain safety, efficacy, and quality, may increase risks of medical and financial harm."

**Why it matters:** This study hands regulators and journalists a number — 27% required a video visit — that will be quoted in every future rulemaking and lawsuit about remote prescribing. For operators who do run real clinical intake, it is also the strongest differentiation argument available right now, and it is documentable.

**Pillar:** 1 and 5 — Regulatory weather / tech and process in the clinic.

## 10. GLP-1 Persistence Nearly Doubled In Three Years

**Category:** RETENTION
**Source:** Journal of Managed Care & Specialty Pharmacy (full text via PubMed Central)
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12948759/
**Date:** JMCP, March 2026, Vol. 32 Issue 3

**The Rundown:** A study of 33,607 commercially insured adults without diabetes initiating high-potency weight-loss GLP-1s found one-year persistence rose from 33.2% in 2021 to 60.9% in the first half of 2024. Adherence improved on the same curve. The authors attribute the gain to resolved supply shortages, better dose-escalation protocols, side-effect management, and lifestyle-program adoption.

**The details:**
- Sample: 33,607 commercially insured adults without diabetes initiating semaglutide (Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Zepbound).
- One-year persistence: 33.2% (2021), 34.1% (2022), 40.2% (2023), 60.9% (1H 2024).
- By drug in 1H 2024: semaglutide 58.6%, tirzepatide 64.8%. Tirzepatide was 64.0% in 2023.
- Mean proportion of days covered (PDC): 52.6% (2021), 51.6% (2022), 57.3% (2023), 72.8% (1H 2024).
- Share reaching ≥80% adherence: 30.2% (2021), 30.7% (2022), 35.9% (2023), 55.5% (1H 2024).
- Persistence definition: no more than a 60-day gap between end of days-supply and the next fill, within 365 days of initiation.
- Authors note real-world persistence remains below clinical-trial levels (>80%).

**Why it matters:** The operational levers the authors credit — dose escalation protocol, side-effect management, structured lifestyle support — are all clinic-controlled, not drug-controlled. That reframes retention from a patient-motivation problem into a protocol-design problem, and gives operators a published benchmark to measure their own 12-month numbers against.

## 13. Meta's Health Ad Rules, In Meta's Own Words

**Category:** ADVERTISING / POLICY
**Source:** Meta Transparency Center — Advertising Standards, Health and Wellness
**URL:** https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/
**Date:** Current standing policy (living document, no publication date on page)

**The Rundown:** Meta's advertising standards separate health and wellness advertising into outright prohibitions and age-gated restrictions. Weight-loss before/after imagery and body-shaming content are banned outright; dietary weight-loss products and supplements are permitted only to 18+ audiences; prescription-drug advertising is routed to a separate policy.

**The details:**
- Prohibited outright: "Side-by-side comparison after the use of a product or transformation for weight loss"; "close up on specific body area by pinching fat"; any messaging featuring "body-shaming" or exploiting "insecurities to conform to certain beauty standards."
- Also prohibited: side-by-side transformation imagery for wrinkle treatment; skin whitening/bleaching products causing permanent color change; genital enhancement procedures and products focused on sexual pleasure.
- Restricted to 18+: "Dietary weight loss products and services such as weight loss pills, or supplements," permitted "without employing negative self-perception tactics."
- Before/after imagery is permitted for cosmetic procedures and anti-aging treatments targeting 18+ when showing "realistic outcomes" without side-by-side comparisons.
- Weight-loss products requiring a prescription are directed to Meta's separate Drugs and Pharmaceuticals ads policy.

**Why it matters:** This is the actual rulebook, not an agency's summary of it — and it explains why so many men's health accounts get rejected without a clear reason. The prohibitions are written around imagery and framing, which means the creative, not the product, is usually what is getting the account flagged.

**Flag:** This is the primary policy source, deliberately chosen over the many agency blog posts on this topic. Those posts circulate specific 2026 enforcement statistics (e.g. "rejection rates spiked 34% in Q1 2026") that are self-described as estimates and are not attributable to Meta. Do not repeat those numbers. Also note: this page does not contain hormone- or testosterone-specific language; TRT advertising restrictions route through the separate Drugs and Pharmaceuticals policy, which was not fetched for this brief.
