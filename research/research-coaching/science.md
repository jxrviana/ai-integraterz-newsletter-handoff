## 1. The testosterone heart warning is gone. The fracture signal is not.

**Summary.** On February 28, 2025 the FDA removed the boxed cardiovascular warning that had sat on testosterone products since 2015, after the 5,246-man TRAVERSE trial found no increase in major adverse cardiac events (7.0% on testosterone vs 7.3% on placebo; HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.17). The same trial, however, recorded *more* fractures in the testosterone group (clinical-fracture HR 1.43; all-fracture HR 1.52), plus higher atrial fibrillation (3.5% vs 2.4%) and acute kidney injury (2.3% vs 1.5%).

**Why it matters to a coach.** This is the single best authority-and-myth-buster item in the set: the "testosterone wrecks your heart" fear that clients arrive with is now formally retired by the regulator, and a coach who can say that with the trial numbers sounds like the most informed person in the room. The nuance is what separates a serious coach from a supplement salesman: TRT is not a bone-protective, not risk-free, and the fracture and rhythm signals are exactly the kind of thing a coach should flag toward the client's physician rather than wave away.

**Source.** Healio Endocrinology, "Removal of boxed warning clears testosterone of heart-related risk, but concerns remain" (Jul 15, 2025): https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20250710/removal-of-boxed-warning-clears-testosterone-of-heartrelated-risk-but-concerns-remain · Primary underlying trial (fractures): NEJM, "Testosterone Treatment and Fractures in Men with Hypogonadism": https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2308836

**Source grade & caveats.** B1 as cited (reputable medical trade outlet reporting an A-grade FDA action and an A-grade NEJM randomized trial; figures corroborated across multiple outlets). TRAVERSE enrolled middle-aged and older men with diagnosed hypogonadism and existing cardiovascular disease or high risk, so the safety read-out applies to *treatment of diagnosed low testosterone*, not recreational or supratherapeutic use. **Almost certain (High confidence)** on the FDA action and the headline MACE result; the fracture finding was unexpected and is single-trial, so **likely (Moderate confidence)** as a durable effect.

**Suggested use.** Lead or second story. Category label: HORMONES / FDA. Strong candidate for the authority slot because it is both reassuring and responsibly caveated.

## 2. Every kilogram a client loses raises his own testosterone about 0.6%.

**Summary.** A 36-month study of 61 adults with obesity (International Journal of Obesity, Jul 2024) found that for each 1 kg of weight a man lost, his total testosterone rose about 0.6% (95% CI 0.2% to 1.0%), independent of age; in women the same weight loss lowered testosterone. Separately, an Endocrine Society ENDO 2025 conference abstract of 110 men on GLP-1 medications reported the share with normal total and free testosterone climbing from 53% to 77% alongside roughly 10% weight loss.

**Why it matters to a coach.** This is the coach's lane, spelled out in numbers: the lever a coach actually controls, fat loss, moves the hormone the client cares about, no prescription required. It reframes "should I go on TRT?" into "let us see what 15 to 20 pounds does to your labs first," which is both better medicine and a longer, stickier coaching engagement.

**Source.** Peer-reviewed: Brzozowska et al., "Sex-differential testosterone response to long-term weight loss," Int J Obes (Jul 16, 2024): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11420080/ · Fresh corroborator (conference abstract): Endocrine Society, ENDO 2025 press release (Jul 14, 2025): https://www.endocrine.org/news-and-advocacy/news-room/endo-annual-meeting/endo-2025-press-releases/portillo-canales-press-release

**Source grade & caveats.** B2 for the direction of effect (weight loss raises endogenous testosterone in men), which is well established across the literature; the precise 0.6%-per-kg magnitude rests on one small observational study (n=61, no randomization) so treat it as illustrative, not a law. The ENDO 2025 figure is a **conference abstract, not peer-reviewed** (grade B3), so cite it as "early data presented at ENDO 2025," never as a settled result. **Very likely (High confidence)** that meaningful weight loss raises men's testosterone; **roughly even chance (Low confidence)** that the exact 0.6%/kg figure replicates.

**Suggested use.** Pairs naturally right after story 1 as the "and here is what you can do without a script" beat. Category label: HORMONES / LIFESTYLE.

## 3. Across 398,716 people, being unfit was the bigger risk than being heavy.

**Summary.** A British Journal of Sports Medicine meta-analysis (Weeldreyer et al., 2024; 20 studies, 398,716 observations, 67% male) found that fit men and women showed no statistically significant increase in all-cause mortality at any BMI, while unfit people carried roughly 1.8 to 3.4 times the mortality risk regardless of weight. For cardiovascular death the picture was partial rather than absolute: fit-but-obese individuals still had a numerically higher (but non-significant) risk, so fitness blunted the danger of extra weight without fully erasing it.

**Why it matters to a coach.** For any client who has stalled on the scale and lost motivation, this is the reframe that keeps him training: the cardiorespiratory fitness he is building is doing more for his lifespan than the last ten pounds. It is a defensible, big-sample answer to the "but I am still overweight, what is the point" objection that coaches hear every week.

**Source.** Weeldreyer et al., "Cardiorespiratory fitness, body mass index and mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis," Br J Sports Med (2024): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11874340/

**Source grade & caveats.** B2 (peer-reviewed meta-analysis in a strong journal). Limitations the coach should not overstate past: fitness was split into a simple fit/unfit cut rather than a dose curve; the studies used BMI, not body composition; and the cohorts skew US and lower on ethnic diversity. The honest line is "fitness strongly attenuates the mortality risk of higher weight," not "fitness cancels obesity." **Very likely (High confidence)** for the all-cause finding; **likely (Moderate confidence)** for the stronger "fitness beats fatness" phrasing given the partial cardiovascular result.

**Suggested use.** Mid-issue authority/reframe story. Category label: LONGEVITY / FITNESS.

## 4. Ninety to 120 minutes of strength work a week tracked with the lowest death risk.

**Summary.** A February 2026 British Journal of Sports Medicine study followed 147,374 people across three long-running cohorts for up to 30 years (35,798 deaths) and found that 90 to 119 minutes per week of strength training was associated with a 13% lower risk of death from any cause, a 19% lower cardiovascular-death risk, and a 27% lower neurological-death risk. Benefit did not keep climbing with more volume, and the association weakened above that window.

**Why it matters to a coach.** This gives a coach a specific, current, large-sample number to anchor programming and to counter the "more is always better" instinct: two focused sessions a week clears most of the longevity benefit. It is the freshest item in the set (February 2026) and lands directly on what a strength coach sells.

**Source.** Lay summary, BMJ Group (Feb 6, 2026): https://bmjgroup.com/90-120-weekly-minutes-of-strength-training-may-be-optimal-for-lowering-death-risk/ · Study record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42230125/

**Source grade & caveats.** B2 (peer-reviewed, very large, long follow-up), but observational and exposure was self-reported, so the authors explicitly state no cause-and-effect can be drawn; certain modalities (calisthenics, Pilates) were excluded and session intensity was not captured. Frame as "associated with," not "causes." **Very likely (High confidence)** the association is real; **likely (Moderate confidence)** that 90 to 120 minutes is a true optimum rather than an artifact of self-report.

**Suggested use.** Strong programming-authority story, easy to turn into a client-facing takeaway. Category label: TRAINING / LONGEVITY.

## 5. Irregular sleep predicted death better than short sleep in 88,975 people.

**Summary.** An eLife study (Cribb et al., Nov 2023) put wrist accelerometers on 88,975 UK Biobank participants and scored sleep-timing consistency; the most irregular sleepers carried a 53% higher all-cause mortality risk and an 88% higher cardiovascular mortality risk than the median, and the association held after accounting for how long people slept. Consistent bed-and-wake timing, not just total hours, tracked with living longer.

**Why it matters to a coach.** Most clients think "sleep" means "get eight hours," and miss that going to bed at wildly different times is its own risk. A coach can turn this into the simplest possible homework, a fixed sleep-and-wake window, and point to a near-90,000-person study for why it beats chasing a duration number.

**Source.** Cribb et al., "Sleep regularity and mortality: a prospective analysis in the UK Biobank," eLife (Nov 23, 2023): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10666928/ · Journal version: https://elifesciences.org/articles/88359

**Source grade & caveats.** B2 (peer-reviewed, large, objective accelerometry). Observational, so causation is not established; accelerometers can misclassify quiet wake as sleep and miss naps. **This is the oldest item in the set (late 2023, roughly 2.7 years).** A companion UK Biobank paper in the journal *Sleep* (Windred et al., 2024) reached the same conclusion, which is why the finding is safe to lead a sleep block with despite the date. **Very likely (High confidence)** the regularity-mortality link is real and reproducible.

**Suggested use.** Recovery/lifestyle story, or the "one thing to change this week" utility beat. Category label: SLEEP / RECOVERY.

## 6. On GLP-1 drugs, up to 39% of the weight lost was muscle.

**Summary.** A November 2025 Healio clinical review reports that in the pivotal trials, about 39% of the weight lost on semaglutide 2.4 mg (STEP-1, NEJM 2021) and about 25% of the weight lost on tirzepatide (SURMOUNT-1, 2025) was lean mass, not fat. The interventions that protect that muscle are the ones a coach already sells: roughly 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight per day and strength training at least three times a week.

**Why it matters to a coach.** This is the most timely and most differentiating story in the set, because a growing share of a men's-health coach's clients are already on Ozempic, Wegovy, or Zepbound, and most have no plan for the muscle they are shedding along with the fat. It hands the coach a clean, evidence-backed pitch: the drug handles appetite, but the coach is what keeps the weight loss from becoming frailty.

**Source.** Healio Endocrinology, "Muscle matters: The challenge of preserving lean mass during obesity treatment" (Nov 18, 2025): https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20251114/muscle-matters-the-challenge-of-preserving-lean-mass-during-obesity-treatment

**Source grade & caveats.** B1/B2 (reputable medical outlet summarizing peer-reviewed trials, including an NEJM trial). One honest nuance the source itself raises: DXA-measured "lean mass" includes body fluid, so the true muscle loss is likely somewhat lower than the 25% to 39% headline; the 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg protein target also lacks a settled reference weight (actual vs corrected). Present the fractions as "lean mass," and note the fluid caveat if space allows. **Almost certain (High confidence)** that GLP-1 weight loss includes substantial lean mass; **very likely (High confidence)** that protein plus resistance training reduces it.

**Suggested use.** Prime candidate for the lead story of issue one given timeliness, and the most natural bridge to a coaching offer. Category label: METABOLIC / GLP-1.

## 7. Creatine's first-week "gain" was mostly water, and a 2025 trial found no extra muscle from it.

**Summary.** A March 2025 randomized trial in *Nutrients* (Desai et al., 63 adults, 5 g/day for 13 weeks) found the early lean-body-mass bump from creatine (about 0.51 kg, appearing mainly in women) was likely water retention, and that over 12 weeks of resistance training the creatine group gained no more lean mass than control (about 2 kg in both). The authors suggest the standard 5 g/day maintenance dose may be too low to add muscle beyond training and call for testing higher doses.

**Why it matters to a coach.** Creatine is the supplement clients ask about most, and this is the intellectually honest version a good coach can teach: creatine is one of the most-studied and generally supportive supplements, but the scale jump in week one is water, not muscle, and one solid 2025 trial found it did not out-build training at the usual dose. Coaching that manages expectations ("your weight will tick up from water, that is normal") builds more trust than hype.

**Source.** Desai et al., "The Effect of Creatine Supplementation on Lean Body Mass with and Without Resistance Training," Nutrients (Mar 19, 2025): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11944689/

**Source grade & caveats.** B3 for this single exploratory RCT (legitimate journal, but n=63, mixed-sex, and it runs against a larger body of meta-analytic evidence that generally favors creatine plus training for fat-free mass). Do **not** let the headline read as "creatine is useless." The accurate framing is: broadly supported supplement, one 2025 trial adds a real caveat about water weight and dose. **Roughly even chance (Low confidence)** that this specific null-for-muscle result holds up against the wider literature; **very likely (High confidence)** that the acute lean-mass bump is largely water. `[OWNER: if you want to cite the countervailing "creatine works" meta-analysis figure, ask for a verified number before publishing.]`

**Suggested use.** Supplement-myth story, or a utility "what your clients get wrong about creatine" teaching block. Category label: SUPPLEMENTS.

## Gaps and caveats

- **Recency profile is honest, not uniform.** Four items are 2025 or 2026 (#4 Feb 2026, #6 Nov 2025, #7 Mar 2025, plus the 2025 ENDO abstract in #2). Item #1's hook is the Feb 2025 FDA action; #3 is 2024; #5 is late 2023 and flagged as such. If the brief demands strict last-12-months-only, #5 and #3 are the two to reconsider, though both are landmark-grade.
- **Two items lean on reputable medical trade summaries (Healio) rather than the primary paper**, because several primary hosts (NEJM, journal PDFs) block automated fetch. The summaries were cross-checked against named trials (TRAVERSE/NEJM, STEP-1/NEJM, SURMOUNT-1). Before publishing a direct quote or an exact statistic from #1 or #6, pull the primary paper to confirm the specific number.
- **Not verified against the primary document:** the exact TRAVERSE atrial-fibrillation percentage (3.5% vs 2.4%) and the STEP-1/SURMOUNT-1 lean-mass fractions were taken from the trade summaries, not the source PDFs. Directionally solid; confirm the decimals if you print them.
- **Observational ceiling.** Items #3, #4, and #5 are association studies. None proves causation, and the newsletter copy must say "associated with" / "tracked with," not "causes." This is both accurate and, for a credible publication, protective.
- **Not searched this pass:** omega-3 and muscle, vitamin D and biological aging, zone-2 cardio, continuous glucose monitoring in non-diabetics, and cold/heat exposure. Any of these could feed issue two.
- **What would change the picks:** a 2025 to 2026 primary paper on natural testosterone optimization (sleep, training, or micronutrients) with a hard number would be a better, fresher #2 than the small n=61 study; and a peer-reviewed (not abstract) GLP-1-plus-resistance-training RCT would upgrade #6 from "expert guidance" to "trial evidence."

## Reserve bench (leads, not yet fully graded — verify before use)

These surfaced during collection and are worth a look for issue two. They are **not** graded findings yet; each needs its primary source fetched and its numbers confirmed.

- **Grip or muscle strength as a longevity vital sign.** A 2025 Mayo Clinic Proceedings analysis suggested muscle *power* (strength times speed) predicts mortality even better than raw strength. Distinct, practical "measure it in the gym" angle. Lead: https://www.mayoclinicproceedings.org/article/S0025-6196(25)00100-4/abstract
- **Time-restricted eating for metabolic syndrome.** A 2024 UCSD/Salk RCT (about 108 adults, 10-hour eating window) reported improved glycemic markers. Real trial, but the eating-window literature is noisy and heavily mis-reported, so handle with care. Lead: https://health.ucsd.edu/news/press-releases/2024-09-30-study-time-restricted-eating-may-improve-health-of-adults-with-metabolic-syndrome/
- **Protein dose ceiling.** Multiple analyses converge on roughly 1.6 g/kg/day maximizing training-driven muscle gains, with little added benefit up to about 2.2 g/kg. Useful evergreen teaching block; the strongest anchor paper is older, so find a 2024 to 2026 review before leading with it.
