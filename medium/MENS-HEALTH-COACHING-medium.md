# Anyone Can Sell a Man Testosterone Now. Being the One He Trusts Is the Whole Business.

*The men's-health market is booming and commoditizing at the same time. Pills, apps, and franchise clinics all sell the shortcut. The coach's edge is the thing the shortcut structurally cannot deliver — and it can be built into a system.*

## The shortcut got industrialized

For most of the last decade, a men's-health coach sold access. You were the person who knew what a real testosterone panel looked like, who understood the difference between "in range" and "optimal," who could point a tired forty-five-year-old toward a protocol he could not assemble on his own. Access was scarce, and scarcity was the business.

That era is closing. In February 2025 the FDA ordered a class-wide labeling change that stripped the boxed cardiovascular warning off every testosterone product, citing the 5,246-man TRAVERSE trial, which found heart-event rates on testosterone essentially level with placebo. Seven months later, Hims launched a men's-health category built on an FDA-approved oral testosterone and closed the year at $2.35 billion in company revenue, up 59 percent, citing its own estimate of twenty million American men with low testosterone. Down the road from your gym, franchised clinics now advertise same-day labs and a walk-out prescription; one chain, Gameday Men's Health, reports more than 400 locations.

Read that as a coach and the point is uncomfortable but clean. The thing you used to sell — access to the protocol — is now a checkout button with a nine-figure ad budget behind it. You cannot outspend that, and you should not try to.

## What the machine cannot sell is the truth

Here is what the checkout button cannot do, and it is the entire opening.

Sit in the places where men actually talk about this and one story repeats almost word for word: my doctor said my labs were normal, but I feel like a different person — foggy, flat, tired by noon. He is dismissed on one side by a physician who shrugs and says "watchful waiting," and oversold on the other by an ad promising masculinity in a vial. He knows something is off. He does not know who to trust. That gap — between the doctor who will not help and the funnel that only wants the sale — is the most valuable real estate in men's health, and neither the pill nor the app can occupy it, because they are the thing he does not trust.

The coach can. Being the honest broker means telling a man the whole truth, including the parts that do not help you close.

It means saying that yes, the FDA removed the heart warning — and that the same trial still recorded more atrial fibrillation in the testosterone group, 3.5 percent against 2.4, plus a fracture signal nobody expected. It means telling a client on a GLP-1 that the drug is doing real work on his appetite, and that in its pivotal trial roughly 39 percent of the weight lost on semaglutide was lean mass, not fat — so without protein and resistance training he is not getting leaner so much as getting smaller and weaker. It means telling the man reaching for a vial that fat loss on its own tends to raise his own testosterone, and that a coach who reads his labs in context is worth more than a subscription that reads nothing.

None of that is a sales script. That is the product. Nuance is what a man cannot get from a checkout page, and it is exactly what he is starving for.

## And the outcome a subscription never delivers

There is a second thing the shortcut cannot sell, and it is the one that actually pays.

A refill ships whether or not the client changed anything. The subscription is indifferent to the outcome; it is designed to renew, not to work. The coach is the only party in the whole market paid to make the change stick — and how that happens is not a mystery. Retention research from the fitness industry found that 87 percent of clients who were onboarded properly, with real goal-setting up front and structured check-ins through the first ninety days, were still active six months later. The operators who earn the most per client are consistently the ones who open with a long assessment and a deliberate on-ramp, not a welcome email and a login.

That is what a man is actually buying. Not the molecule — he can get the molecule anywhere now — but the twelve months of someone watching, adjusting, and refusing to let him quit in week three. The pill has no opinion about whether he succeeds. The coach's entire existence is the opinion.

## The honest broker's real problem

So the positioning is settled. In a market where the product got safer on paper, the audience being marketed to got larger, and a $2 billion competitor walked in, the coach who wins is the honest broker: nuance the funnel cannot fake, and an outcome the subscription was never built to deliver.

There is only one problem, and it is not talent. It is that the man who needs you cannot tell you apart from the crowd. "Coach" is an unprotected word, the field is filling fast — professional coaching crossed 122,974 practitioners and $5.34 billion in revenue in 2025, up 15 percent in two years — and every scam headline makes the next prospect more skeptical before you have said a word. Being right is not the same as being found. You can be the most honest operator in your city and still lose the client to the funnel with the bigger budget, for no reason other than that he never heard your name.

That is a systems problem. Systems are buildable.
