# Men's Health Clinics Can't Really Advertise. Here's What They Build Instead.

*Platform rules push hormone and peptide businesses toward referral, creator and partnership channels by default. This is how those channels actually get built.*

## The constraint most categories never have to think about

Run a TRT clinic, a peptide or telehealth brand, or a coaching practice in men's health, and you learn the same thing early. The ad account is not something you can build a company on.

Ad platforms restrict claims about hormones, prescription treatments and peptides. Creative gets rejected. Accounts get restricted. The language that would most clearly describe what the business does is often the exact language that trips the review.

The common response is to hire someone who writes compliant ads. That works in a narrow way. The ceiling stays low, the account stays fragile, and a policy update nobody warned you about can take the channel away in a morning.

The more durable response is to stop treating paid traffic as the main engine and build the three channels policy cannot reach: referral partners, niche creators, and podcast placements.

None of these are new. What has changed is how deliberately operators work them — as a system with named targets and follow-up that survives a busy week, rather than networking that happens when somebody remembers.

## Channel one: the businesses already sitting across from your patient

Someone is already in the room with your future patient at the moment he decides something has to change. It is rarely you.

It is his primary care physician. A urologist. The chiropractor treating the back that never fully recovered. The physical therapist he has been seeing for months. A dietitian or nutritionist. The trainer at his gym. The med spa he already pays for. The benefits lead at the company that employs him.

Those are power partners. The referral arrives pre-trusted, which is the entire point. He is not comparing your clinic against three others — he is taking his physical therapist's word for it.

Most operators ruin this by opening with the ask. "Would you be willing to send patients our way" is a request for a favor from someone who owes you nothing.

The first move is the opposite. Lead with something that helps them: a men's health talk for their members, a co-branded offer their clients get, a standing willingness to send your patients back the other direction. Reciprocity comes first, and referrals follow it in that order.

Work the list in a deliberate sequence rather than alphabetically. Score each target on three things — how much their customer base overlaps with yours, how many people move through their door in a month, and how reachable the real decision-maker is. Start at the top and work down.

## Channel two: creators who already hold the room

The second channel is creators — fitness, longevity, and health-optimization voices, plus the local figures who hold a room without holding a large following. Gym owners. Coaches. Former athletes who are known in one town.

Run a fit test before spending anything. Is the audience local, or genuinely in-category? Does it skew male and middle-aged? Does the creator already talk about training, health, and self-improvement, so that a men's health mention reads as on-topic instead of a swerve?

Three yeses and it is worth pursuing. Fewer than that and it isn't, no matter how good the follower count looks.

The first move here is a collaboration that serves their audience rather than a paid post that serves you. A Q&A. An episode built around what their own bloodwork actually said. A perk their community gets and nobody else does.

Give every creator a unique code or landing page from day one. Without it you will never know which ones produce, and you will keep renewing the ones that don't.

## Channel three: the long conversation

Podcasts are the third channel, and they stay underused because they look slow.

A men's health business sells trust before it sells anything else. A long conversation in which the owner or medical lead teaches — what labs actually show, how protocols get adjusted, what recovery looks like over time — reaches the man searching "low energy" late at night in a way no ad slot does.

Two lanes matter. Health-optimization and longevity shows for the clinical lead. Local business and community shows for the owner.

Pitch the host on what their listeners get, never on who you are. Name the segment, offer a few angles and a one-sheet, and make saying yes easy.

Then close every appearance the same way: one soft call to action and a trackable link. The episode keeps working long after the recording, and the clips keep working on other platforms.

## The part that actually breaks

These channels fail for an unglamorous reason. They are slow to build and easy to let lapse.

A partner meets you, likes you, agrees in principle — and then nobody follows up, because the week got busy and there is no system holding the thread. A creator posts, a few people click, and no one calls them back the same day. That is where most of the loss happens, and it has nothing to do with the strategy.

So follow-up has to be automated, not remembered. Every new lead gets contacted fast. Every partner gets a check-in on a schedule. Every creator and podcast carries a tracking link, so production is attributable by name.

That is the honest version of the method. The channels are simple. The consistency is the hard part.
