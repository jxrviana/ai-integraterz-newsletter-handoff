## 1. The Peptide Panel Voted. Six Of Seven Got Through.

**Category:** FDA / PEPTIDES
**Source:** TIME, "An FDA Committee Just Voted in Favor of Peptides" (time.com/article/2026/07/23/fda-committee-peptides/)
**Corroborating sources:** STAT News (statnews.com/2026/07/23/fda-panel-okays-peptides-compound-pharmacies-bpc-157-kpv/); Pharmaceutical Executive (pharmexec.com/view/fda-votes-loosen-restrictions-four-peptides); RAPS (raps.org/resource/fda-advisory-committee-backs-two-controversial-peptides.html)
**Date:** PCAC meeting held July 23-24, 2026; coverage July 23-24, 2026
**Verified:** July 30, 2026
**Sourcing & confidence:** Outcome graded **B1** (four independent reputable outlets agree). Confidence **High** that the panel recommended six of seven. Confidence **Low/uncertain** on what FDA ultimately does with the recommendation.

**The Rundown:** The FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee met July 23-24 and recommended adding six of the seven peptides on its docket to the Section 503A bulks list, the list that governs what state-licensed pharmacies may legally compound. The panel backed BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax, and Epitalon. It rejected only emideltide (DSIP). The votes overruled FDA's own scientists, who had recommended against all seven. The recommendations are non-binding and change nothing about what is legal today.

**The details:**
- Day 1 (July 23): BPC-157, KPV, and TB-500 each passed 8-6 with one abstention; MOTS-c passed 7-5 with two abstentions (Pharmaceutical Executive; BPC-157's 8-6-1 also confirmed by TIME).
- Day 2 (July 24): Semax and Epitalon were recommended; emideltide (DSIP) was the panel's only rejection, failing 6-7 with one abstention (TIME).
- The reporting split some readers will notice: outlets that filed on July 23 covered "four peptides" (the Day 1 votes), while coverage of the full two-day meeting shows six of seven. Both describe the same event at different points.
- FDA staff briefing documents had recommended against listing all seven; the committee agreed with staff only on emideltide (STAT, RAPS, TIME).
- Process from here: a positive PCAC vote is not FDA action. It opens formal notice-and-comment rulemaking (a proposed rule, a comment period, then a final rule). Pharmaceutical Executive put the realistic distance at 8 to 12 months before any pharmacy would have legal authority to compound. A final rule realistically runs into 2027.
- Conflict-of-interest angle worth flagging to operators: STAT and NBC News both reported that a majority of the panelists who voted yes had ties to the peptide industry. That is those outlets' characterization, and it is the kind of detail that shapes how much weight FDA gives a non-binding recommendation.

**Why it matters:** For any clinic or brand whose peptide line has sat in regulatory limbo since 2023, the panel just pointed the door toward opening. The trap is reading the vote as permission. It is not. Nothing about BPC-157 or TB-500 became legal to compound this week, and an operator who restocks or rewrites marketing on the strength of a non-binding recommendation is front-running a rule that does not exist yet and may take a year to arrive.

**Pillar:** 1, Regulatory weather.

## 2. FDA Still Wants GLP-1s Off The Bulk List. Comments Are In.

**Category:** FDA / GLP-1
**Source:** Orrick, "FDA Moves to Shut the Door on Large-Scale Compounding of GLP-1 Drugs" (orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/05/FDA-Moves-to-Shut-the-Door-on-Large-Scale-Compounding-of-GLP1-Drugs), re-fetched July 30, 2026, loads, facts hold
**Corroborating sources:** FDA press announcement "FDA Proposes to Exclude Semaglutide, Tirzepatide, and Liraglutide on 503B Bulks List" (fda.gov, referenced; page blocks automated fetch); Federal Register doc 2026-08552, published May 1, 2026; NABP and Drug Topics coverage
**Date:** Proposal April 30, 2026; Federal Register May 1, 2026; comment period closed June 29, 2026
**Verified:** July 30, 2026
**Sourcing & confidence:** **B1.** The proposal itself is an A-grade primary document reported consistently across sources. Confidence **High** that it remains a proposal in review; confidence **Moderate** on the timing of any final rule (none is scheduled publicly).

**The Rundown:** FDA's April 30 proposal to exclude semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from the 503B bulks list is still just that, a proposal. The public comment window closed June 29, 2026, and as of this refresh the agency is reviewing comments. No final rule has issued. The stated basis is unchanged: FDA finds no clinical need for outsourcing facilities to compound these three drugs from bulk substance.

**The details:**
- The three drugs named: semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide.
- 503B outsourcing facilities take the hardest hit. If the rule is finalized, they lose the legal basis to compound these at scale, since neither the bulks list nor a shortage designation would authorize it (Orrick).
- 503A pharmacies are not directly changed by this proposal but remain bound by existing prohibitions on copying commercially available drugs (Orrick).
- Comment period: April 30 to June 29, 2026. Status as of July 30: comments under review, no final action announced.

**Why it matters:** The clock on this one did not stop when comments closed. It moved to FDA's desk. Any men's-health or weight-loss program still built on bulk-compounded GLP-1 supply is running a revenue line whose legal footing FDA has publicly proposed to remove. The finalization date is unknown, which is exactly why the pivot work (branded partnerships, or exiting the category) belongs on the calendar now rather than the week the rule drops.

**Pillar:** 1, Regulatory weather.

## 3. The Telehealth Prescribing Rules Still Expire December 31.

**Category:** DEA / TELEHEALTH
**Source:** Holland & Knight, "DEA and HHS Extend Telemedicine Prescribing Flexibilities Through 2026" (hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/01/dea-and-hhs-extend-telemedicine-prescribing-flexibilities-through-2026), re-fetched July 30, 2026, loads, facts hold
**Corroborating sources:** DEA press release Dec 31, 2025 (dea.gov, referenced; blocks automated fetch); Federal Register doc 2025-24123, published Dec 31, 2025; McDermott+Consulting coverage
**Date:** Rule effective January 1 to December 31, 2026; H&K analysis January 13, 2026
**Verified:** July 30, 2026
**Sourcing & confidence:** **B1.** The extension is an A-grade primary rule reported consistently. Confidence **High** that the flexibilities run through Dec 31, 2026 and no permanent rule has landed. Confidence **Low** on whether a permanent rule arrives before the deadline (roughly even chance).

**The Rundown:** The fourth temporary extension of pandemic-era telemedicine prescribing rules is still the operating law: practitioners can prescribe Schedule II-V controlled substances by telemedicine without an initial in-person exam, through December 31, 2026. No permanent replacement rule has been finalized. Testosterone and its esters are Schedule III, so this rule governs telehealth TRT prescribing directly.

**The details:**
- Effective window: January 1 through December 31, 2026. Covers Schedule II-V (Holland & Knight).
- No new documentation, registration, or technology requirement was imposed by the extension.
- DEA states it intends to issue final regulations before the 2026 deadline and is still reviewing comments on the proposed permanent rules (Holland & Knight). Both the agency and its counsel describe the extension as a bridge to avoid a "telemedicine cliff."
- Two narrower final rules did take effect December 31, 2025 (one on buprenorphine for opioid use disorder, one on VA continuity of care), but the broad temporary flexibility remains the most permissive pathway during the transition (Holland & Knight).

**Why it matters:** Every telehealth TRT business is operating on a permission slip with roughly five months left on it, and the replacement rule is not written. A permanent framework could add platform-level or prescriber-level registration requirements that do not exist today. This is the single largest structural unknown on the calendar for remote-prescribing men's-health businesses, and it has a hard date.

**Pillar:** 1, Regulatory weather.

## 4. FDA Sent A Second Wave Of GLP-1 Marketing Warning Letters.

**Category:** FDA / ADVERTISING
**Source (March wave):** Foley & Lardner, "GLP-1 Compliance: FDA Targets Telehealth Marketing in 30 New Warning Letters" (foley.com/p/102mmr0/...), re-fetched July 30, 2026, loads, facts hold
**Source (June wave):** RAPS, "FDA cites companies for CGMP violations, telehealth firms for illegally marketing GLP-1 drugs" (raps.org/resource/fda-cites-companies-for-cgmp-violations-telehealth-firms-for-illegally-marketing-glp-1-drugs.html)
**Corroborating sources:** FDA press announcements (fda.gov, referenced; block automated fetch); Sheppard Mullin and McDermott coverage of the June round
**Date:** First wave issued March 3, 2026 (Foley published March 12); second wave issued around June 8, posted June 16, 2026 (RAPS)
**Verified:** July 30, 2026
**Sourcing & confidence:** **B1.** Two independent reputable sources for two separate enforcement waves. Confidence **High** on both waves and the violation categories, and **High** that this is now a sustained enforcement pattern rather than a one-off.

**The Rundown:** The 30 warning letters from March were not the end of it. In June 2026, FDA posted a second wave to about 25 telehealth companies over the same conduct: marketing copy for compounded GLP-1 products that implied FDA approval or equivalence to the branded drugs, and branding that obscured who actually compounded the product. The letters target website language and funnels, not the act of compounding.

**The details:**
- March 3, 2026: 30 warning letters. Violation categories cited by FDA (Foley): putting the telehealth firm's own brand on labels in a way that suggests it compounds the drug when it does not; marketing that fails to qualify for 503A or 503B exemptions; promotional claims implying FDA has approved or evaluated the compounded product.
- Specific offending phrases FDA quoted back at companies (Foley): "Generic Zepbound," "Contains the active pharmaceutical ingredient in Wegovy," "This FDA-approved treatment promotes safe, effective weight loss," and "FDA approved active pharmaceutical ingredients."
- June 2026: about 25 more warning letters, issued around June 8 and posted June 16, for false or misleading claims about compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide (and liraglutide in one case), including claims implying sameness with FDA-approved products and branding that hides the actual compounder (RAPS). Companies were given 15 business days to respond.
- FDA's stated escalation path across both waves: failure to correct may lead to enforcement including seizure or injunction, and foreign firms' products may be detained at the border (Foley).

**Why it matters:** Two waves in four months is the signal. This is a marketing-copy enforcement lane, which means the exposure sits with whoever writes the website, the ad, and the funnel, not the clinician. Any operator still describing a compounded product by reference to a brand-name drug is using the exact phrasing FDA has now quoted back at 55 companies.

**Pillar:** 1, Regulatory weather, with direct read-through to pillar 4 (acquisition/marketing).

## 5. Med Spas Are Still Selling Compounded GLP-1s. A Study Named The Weak Link.

**Category:** COMPLIANCE / MARKET
**Source:** Healio, "Compounded semaglutide, tirzepatide continued to be sold after shortages ended" (healio.com/news/endocrinology/20260717/...), re-fetched July 30, 2026, loads, facts hold
**Corroborating source:** University of Colorado Anschutz newsroom, "Study Finds Some Medical Spas Still Selling Compounded Weight-Loss Drugs From Pharmacies With Regulatory Concerns" (news.cuanschutz.edu/news-stories/compounded-glp1-regulatory-concerns)
**Underlying study:** JAMA Health Forum, published July 17, 2026; DOI 10.1001/jamahealthforum.2026.2207; lead author Michael J. DiStefano, PhD, MBE (CU Anschutz)
**Date:** Published July 17, 2026; fieldwork August to October 2025
**Verified:** July 30, 2026
**Sourcing & confidence:** Study provenance and headline findings **B1** (confirmed by CU Anschutz, the source institution, plus Healio, reporting a peer-reviewed JAMA Health Forum paper). The exact percentages are drawn from Healio's reading of the paper and are effectively single-secondary-sourced; confidence **High** on the finding, **Moderate** on any single decimal figure. The JAMA paper itself sits behind the journal and was not read directly.

**The Rundown:** A secret-shopper study of brick-and-mortar weight-loss clinics and med spas in Oklahoma and West Virginia found the overwhelming majority still selling compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide months after the shortage-era enforcement grace period ended. The study's sharper contribution is on the supply side: it profiled the pharmacies filling these orders and found credential gaps.

**The details:**
- 75 businesses confirmed selling compounded medications; 92.0% offered compounded semaglutide, 86.7% offered compounded tirzepatide; one sold an unnamed triple-receptor agonist (Healio).
- Of 23 supplying pharmacies identified: 95.6% held active state licenses, 13% were FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facilities, and 19% of state-licensed facilities lacked sterile compounding authorization (Healio). CU Anschutz corroborates that several suppliers lacked required sterile-compounding licensing or had recent disciplinary actions or FDA warning letters.
- 58.7% sold compounded medications with additives, vitamin B most common (Healio).
- Staffing: nurse practitioners or physician assistants at 56% of businesses; 69.3% required an in-person visit (Healio).
- Fieldwork ran August to October 2025, after FDA's shortage-era enforcement deadlines for compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide had passed (CU Anschutz).
- Researcher guidance for buyers: do not purchase from vendors unwilling to disclose the supplying pharmacy's name (Healio, prior-round reporting).

**Why it matters:** This paper is the map regulators and plaintiffs' lawyers read next, and it names the weak link out loud: the supplying pharmacy, where nearly one in five state-licensed facilities lacked sterile-compounding authorization. Any operator who cannot name their compounder and produce its credentials is carrying someone else's regulatory risk on their own P&L.

**Pillar:** 1 and 3, Regulatory weather / clinical-product developments.
