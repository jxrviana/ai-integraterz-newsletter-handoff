MONETIZATION
💵 Small Shows Convert Nearly As Well As Big Ones

The Short Version: Magellan AI, which sells podcast advertising analytics, published its first Podcast Measurement Benchmark Report — campaigns run January 1 to March 31, 2026, measured through its own attribution pixel, campaign count undisclosed. Across the set, 2.29% of unique reached listeners visited an advertiser’s site, 9.74% of those became leads, and 5.22% purchased. The breakdown by show rank is the part worth reading.

The Details:

  • Purchase conversion by show rank: top 500 shows 5.20%, mid-tier (501–3000) 5.38%, long-tail (3000+) 5.23%.
  • Response rate does scale with size — 2.29% for the top 500 against 1.63% for the long tail. Conversion does not.
  • Host-read ads pulled 2.45% response, against 1.93% for programmatic and 1.51% for produced non-host-read spots.
  • Video beat audio-only on every measure: 2.49% response versus 1.39% RSS-only, and 11.27% lead conversion versus 5.65%.

Why It Matters: Prestige placements get sold on the top-500 name. This data prices the rest of the list: the long tail closes purchases at 5.23% against the top 500’s 5.20%, so a mid-tier booking is not a consolation prize. It is the argument for widening a client’s target list instead of queueing behind everyone else for the same handful of shows.

GUESTS
📋 What Hosts Actually Want From A Guest

The Short Version: Buzzsprout surveyed 271 podcast hosts on how they choose guests. Topic relevance came first at 87.8%, guest expertise second at 62.0%. A guest’s audience size finished eighth of nine at 14.4% — behind a clear episode idea, a timely angle, prior guest experience, a personalised pitch and a referral.

The Details:

  • The full ranking: topic relevance 87.8%, guest expertise 62.0%, clear episode idea 34.3%, timely angle 26.6%, prior guest experience 24.0%, personalised pitch 18.5%, referral 17.7%, audience size 14.4%, simple logistics 10.7%.
  • Where hosts find guests: personal network 69.7% — more than double any other channel.
  • Cold email pitches and referrals from past guests tie at 28.0%, level with social media at 28.4%.
  • Paid routes trail: guest platforms 22.5%, PR agencies 19.9%. Online communities came last at 11.8%.

Why It Matters: This is a pitch checklist ranked by the people who answer the pitches. Topic relevance and expertise decide the booking; a guest’s audience size sits eighth of nine. A client with a modest following is less of a handicap than it feels on a call. Worth sitting with: only 19.9% of these hosts said they find guests through PR agencies.

PODCAST TECH
🔗 SpeakerAgent AI Matches Guests To Podcasts

The Short Version: SpeakerAgent AI is a new site that uses AI to find a person’s best-matched podcasts. A guest profile goes in — topic, expertise, the talking points they can carry — and a ranked list of shows comes back, ordered by how well the guest fits the show rather than by how large its audience is. It addresses the list-building stage of a placement, before any pitch is written.

The Details:

  • The input is a guest profile — topic, expertise and talking points — rather than a keyword search of a directory.
  • Shows are ranked on fit with that specific guest rather than on raw audience size.
  • The output is a shortlist of podcasts judged likely to book the guest.
  • The matching stands in for the show-by-show research an agency would otherwise do by hand.

Why It Matters: Building the target list is the manual part of a placement workflow — one show at a time, from directories and charts, before a single pitch goes out. A tool that ranks shows on fit rather than size sorts against the same criterion hosts say they book on, and it moves that research off the account team.

PLATFORMS
📺 YouTube Is Now The Podcast Platform

The Short Version: Edison Research’s Infinite Dial 2026, fielded in January, put monthly podcast consumption at 58% of Americans aged 12 and over — 167 million people, an all-time high. Among weekly podcast consumers, 39% say they use YouTube most often, against 20% for Spotify and 11% for Apple Podcasts.

The Details:

  • 45% of Americans 12+ (130 million) consumed a podcast in the last week; 80% (230 million) have ever listened to or watched one.
  • 57% of podcast consumers both listen to and watch podcasts. Only 21% listen exclusively; 2% watch exclusively.
  • The 39/20/11 platform split comes from Broadcast Dialogue and SiriusXM Media reporting Edison’s March presentation — Edison’s own public summary page does not publish it.
  • Sample: 2,050 respondents aged 12+, SSRS Opinion Panel, random-digit-dial plus address-based sampling, weighted to the U.S. 12+ census.

Why It Matters: A client’s appearance now has two lives, and 39% of weekly consumers reach podcasts through YouTube most often. Before a show goes on a target list, ask whether it publishes full video, where the clips run and who owns them. A placement reads very differently to a client when the episode is findable by search and recommendation.

GROWTH
📊 What A Good Show Actually Gets

The Short Version: Buzzsprout updated its public download benchmarks in June 2026, drawn from 113,575 active podcasts on its own platform and roughly 82.8 million monthly downloads. The median episode — the line between the top and bottom half of everything published there — gets 27 downloads in its first seven days.

The Details:

  • 27 downloads in seven days puts an episode in the top 50%. 98 downloads puts it in the top 25%.
  • The top 10% is 412 downloads in seven days; the top 5% is 1,015.
  • The top 1% is 4,605 downloads — roughly 170 times the median.
  • Figures comply with IAB Podcast Measurement Technical Guidelines 2.2 and describe Buzzsprout’s hosted base, not the whole industry.

Why It Matters: A client asking what a booking is worth deserves a real number, not a rate card. On Buzzsprout’s base, 27 downloads in seven days is the median episode and 412 puts one in the top 10%. Ask a show for its per-episode figure before it goes on the list, and set the client’s expectation before the recording rather than after the report.

GUESTS
🧾 Almost Nobody Charges Guests

The Short Version: Podseeker, which sells podcast pitching software, analysed 16,590 real pitch threads from its own customer base in May 2026 to settle whether guesting has quietly become pay-to-play. It has not. 99.4% of appearances involved no fee at all; a fee was mentioned by 105 hosts, or 0.6% of the set.

The Details:

  • Where fees do exist, smaller shows charge $49–$100, usually framed as a production or feature fee.
  • Mid-tier shows that charge ask $200–$500; larger ones $1,000–$2,500, typically requiring an in-studio recording.
  • From Podseeker’s separate pitch dataset: 8,757 pitches sent through the platform produced a 3.2% booking rate — roughly 31 pitches per booking.
  • Podseeker states the caveat itself: this is one platform’s customer base, not an industry census, and it reflects shows that already receive pitched guest inquiries.

Why It Matters: A fee request is the exception, not the market — 99.4% of appearances in this set involved no fee at all. When a show asks, it is worth establishing what the money buys and disclosing it to the client. The other number is the workload: a 3.2% booking rate means roughly 31 pitches per placement.

THE PLAYBOOK
 
How To Get Clients Booked On Shows Worth Being On

Every agency with a placement record built it the same boring way. There is no version of this that runs on relationships alone.

1. Build the target list around one client, not around the charts. The shows worth pitching are the ones already booking guests who look like this client. Pull the guest histories from five or six shows in the client’s category, add the shows their competitors have appeared on, and add anything the client’s own customers have mentioned by name. Then prioritise: fit first, reachability second, chart position never — 69.7% of hosts in the Buzzsprout survey find guests through a personal network, so any show where someone on your team has a real connection goes to the top of the list. First move: open a sheet, list twenty shows pulled from three adjacent shows’ guest histories, and mark the five you could reach without a cold email.

2. Pitch the host on their audience, not your client’s résumé. A pitch that gets a yes answers four things in under 150 words: why this show, why this guest specifically, what the host’s listeners get out of the episode, and exactly what you need and how long it takes. Name the episode you want made, not the client you want placed. And be straight about size — the same survey ranks a guest’s audience size 14.4%, eighth of nine booking criteria, and no dataset in our research showed that a notable guest lifts downloads. Fit and visible preparation are what these hosts said they weight. First move: rewrite your standard pitch so the first sentence is about the host’s audience and the last sentence is a date, a duration and a link.

3. Run it as a system, not as a scramble. At a 3.2% booking rate it takes roughly 31 pitches to land one placement, which is a workload, not a mood. Batch the outreach into one standing sitting a week per client, follow up exactly twice — seven days and fourteen days out — then stop and move on. Keep a four-column pipeline (pitched / replied / booked / recorded) for each client so you can see a placement gap forming six weeks before the client sees it. First move: put a weekly outreach block in the calendar for the next eight weeks and treat it as a client meeting.

Step one is the part software can now do: match a guest profile to the shows most likely to book them.
