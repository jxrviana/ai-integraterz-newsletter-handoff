Most local partnerships begin with a request: Can you send me business? That is understandable. It is also usually the wrong opening question.

The Principle

Jay Abraham’s Strategy of Preeminence starts somewhere more useful. The job is to become the person who sees the client’s situation most clearly, then helps improve it before asking to be paid for the help. In practice, that means falling in love with the client, not the product. It means looking for the hidden assets already around the table: trust, access, knowledge, timing, and relationships.

Preeminence is not being the loudest expert in the room. It is taking responsibility for the other party’s outcome before you take responsibility for your own revenue.

The Balance

A Real Local Example

The Portsmouth Partnership describes itself as a group of volunteer business, professional, and civic leaders committed to Portsmouth’s economic development. On its current site, coworking is listed among its active programs, alongside PortsEvents and leadership training.

The point is not to assume that a coworking space automatically creates a profitable partner channel. We do not have evidence for that outcome. The useful lesson is the structure of the opportunity: a place designed to bring people, businesses, and civic energy into closer contact can be treated as a distribution asset, not merely as square footage.

A preeminence-minded operator would not walk in with a commission sheet first. They would ask: What are members trying to accomplish after they leave this room? Which needs recur? Which introductions would save them time? Which local providers already have credibility with those members? Then they would design a small, useful introduction process around those answers.

Your Monday Move

Map the next problem, not the next sale.
1. Ask one local partner: “After someone uses your service, what is the next problem they usually need help solving?”
2. Write down the answer.
3. Make one useful introduction before you mention your program.

That is the first test of whether the relationship has real value.

Why It Pays

The commercial case for trust is not a reason to rush the relationship. Alignable surveyed more than 7,500 small-business owners. 85% said word of mouth was their number-one source of new customers. Nielsen found that 88% of people trust recommendations from people they know more than any other form of advertising.

A Journal of Marketing study by Schmitt, Skiera, and Van den Bulte found referred customers carried 16% to 25% higher lifetime value. That is a reason to measure a referral channel carefully, not a promise that every referral will outperform every other source.

The wider market is paying attention too. eMarketer estimates US affiliate-marketing spend at about $13.81 billion in 2026, up roughly 11.3%. Forrester reports that about 67% of B2B companies expect partner revenue to grow 30% or more. The lesson is balance, measurement, and a channel that earns its place.
