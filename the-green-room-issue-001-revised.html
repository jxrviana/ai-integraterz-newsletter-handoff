Omnicom Merges Golin and Ketchum Into a Single Entity
Image source: original photography for The Green Room. Wide tracking shot across an open-plan PR agency office at 8:15 AM, early morning light.
THE SHORT VERSION
In February 2026, Omnicom Public Relations Group announced it would merge Golin and Ketchum, two of its flagship PR agencies, into a combined entity with integrated offerings across brand communications, health, and social and digital innovation. The restructuring is part of a broader holding company effort to consolidate agency brands, reduce operational overhead, and present a unified front to clients.
THE DETAILS
• The merger was announced on February 12, 2026. Golin, which was with IPG prior to the merger, and Ketchum from Omnicom, "will merge to create a single, premier brand communications agency with integrated offerings across brand communications, health, and social and digital innovation."
• "Porter Novelli will become a dedicated brand within FleishmanHillard, connecting teams and shared offerings to give clients faster access to deeper expertise globally."
• The planned changes "will be implemented in phases through 2026." During the transition, "clients in APAC will continue to work with the agency brands they know, with continuity of day-to-day teams and relationship leadership prioritised."
• Weber Shandwick, MMC, and OPR's public affairs firms continue to operate under existing leadership with no announced changes.
WHY IT MATTERS
This is not just another holding company reorganization. Even the largest PR networks with decades of independent identity are no longer safe from consolidation pressure. For independent agency owners, the merger creates both threat and opportunity: clients seeking dedicated senior attention may look beyond the consolidated networks.

BILLING
AI Is Forcing PR Agencies to Rethink the Billable Hour
Image source: original photography for The Green Room. Tight shot of a PR director's workspace the night before a major pitch, printed decks and handwritten notes across a conference table.
THE SHORT VERSION
AI tools like ChatGPT are completing tasks that used to take hours in minutes, and PR agency leaders are now openly questioning whether the billable hour can survive. Michelle Olson, chief client officer at Lambert by LLYC, says the moment is here for firms to rethink not only how they bill clients but how they prove their value. The conversation about replacing hourly billing has been running for 20 years. AI is forcing it to a conclusion.
THE DETAILS
• "Tools like ChatGPT are completing tasks that used to take hours in minutes so firms may want to consider how that will affect hourly billing," reports PR Daily. Research that once consumed billable hours now happens in seconds.
• "It's really about viewing this opportunity to highlight that we're much more than just 'doers' of tasks," said Michelle Olson, Lambert's chief client officer and a fellow at PRSA. "If we have a retainer-type arrangement with our clients, we still build an hourly rate into that retainer."
• "I think the reason for the use of billable hours is that we've either been scared of or can't define what success looks like," said Aaron Kwittken, founder and CEO of PRophet and a former agency leader. The value billing model, based on impact rather than time, has been discussed for decades but rarely adopted at scale.
• Olson noted that a quality pitch to the Wall Street Journal may only take a five-minute phone call, or about $5 on a $100-per-hour rate, but the value of that placement could be "priceless" to a brand. The disconnect between time billed and value delivered is becoming harder to ignore.
WHY IT MATTERS
The retainer model is built on billable hours. If AI compresses the time required to deliver core services, agencies that bill by the hour face a direct revenue compression. The firms that figure out value-based pricing before their clients demand it will own the next decade. The ones that wait will find themselves defending $100-per-hour rates for work that takes minutes.

Everyone Wants a Referral Channel. Almost Nobody Builds the Part That Makes It Real.
Image source: original photography for The Green Room. Two professionals at a sunlit cafe table, one gesturing with his phone, candid and unposed.
THE SHORT VERSION
A detailed essay by a service business operator who reverse-engineered a client referral system that now generates over 20 percent of new business without paid acquisition or cold outreach. The piece is not about PR agencies specifically, but the mechanics transfer directly. Most professional service firms treat referrals as a hope. They assume good work will be rewarded with word of mouth, and when it is not, they blame the market. Referral volume is a systems problem. You need a defined trigger moment, a near-zero-friction handoff mechanism, and a feedback loop.
THE DETAILS
• Define your trigger moment. In a PR agency, this might be the day a client gets a major placement or wins an award. That is when they are most likely to refer you, and most agencies never ask.
• Reduce the friction. "Let me know if you know anyone who needs PR" is not an ask. "Could you introduce me to the CMO at a company you know is hiring for communications help? I will send you two lines you can forward" is an ask.
• Close the loop. When someone refers you, tell them what happened. Most referrers hear nothing and eventually stop sending people.
• The full system is built on the Power of Eight framework: operationalize tracking, structure the terms, and automate follow-through. In a world where affiliate spend is forecast at $13.81 billion in 2026, the agencies that treat referrals as a product, not a hope, will capture outsized share.

Can Specialized PR Firms Survive the Consolidation Wave?
Image source: original photography for The Green Room. Narrow hallway behind a conference venue, a heavy door labeled with a small brass plate reading PRESS, dramatic backlight.
THE SHORT VERSION
"The global communications industry experienced a seismic shift at the end of 2024 as three of the world's largest agencies, Edelman, Omnicom and IPG, implemented large-scale organizational changes focused on financial and operational efficiency." Edelman phased out several boutique and specialty firms, including Edible, Revere, Salutem, Mustache, EGA and Delta. The question now facing the industry: can specialized firms survive when holding companies are consolidating brands and cutting overhead?
THE DETAILS
• "Operating one of those boutique-style brands requires separate staffing, additional overhead and everything that goes with standing up a separate brand, including the cost of a separate CEO," said Kim Sample, president of the PR Council.
• "I think the boutique model is sustainable because you have a need for specialists, you have a need for generalists," said Jacqueline Babb, a senior lecturer at Northwestern University. "The boutique firm, if it's by itself, gives you the specialists; if it's within a broader firm, it gives you specialties and generalists. I don't think that need goes away."
• "Clients really want that integrated approach, and they don't want to have multiple agencies they need to coordinate," said Annie Pace Scranton, founder and CEO of Pace Public Relations. "There's a ton of opportunity for the larger agencies, existing clients to be pitched and told about the specialty services of this new agency as a means for their current client base."
• "Clients really appreciate senior talent," Sample said. "But they're asking for more while expecting lower costs. That creates a lot of pressure on firms of all sizes." Sample also noted that investments in AI and generative technology are reshaping firm budgets: "In this age where we're having to invest a lot in technology, generative AI being a big piece of it, it's hard to justify all these different costs unless you've got a very clear reason for doing so."
WHY IT MATTERS
The consolidation wave is not just about the big networks. It is reshaping expectations across the entire industry. Independent agencies that position themselves as specialized alternatives are seeing increased inbound interest, but they also face pressure to show measurable ROI. "Because digital marketing has provided so much direct attribution for us, we're trying to seek it in other areas as well," Babb said. "PR firms are starting to see the effects of that, and so they are having to show ROI in a more meaningful way."

THE PLAYBOOK
ONE USEFUL MOVE
The Client Satisfaction Audit (Agency Edition)
Step 1: List every active client. Next to each name, write down the date of the last time someone at the partner or director level had a non-billable, non-deliverable conversation with them.
Step 2: Flag every client where that date is more than 60 days ago. These are your at-risk accounts.
Step 3: Schedule a 20-minute call with each flagged client this week. Ask three questions: "What is working well?" "What could be better?" "What would make you a hero internally this quarter?"
Step 4: For the clients who tell you what would make them a hero, build a one-page scope addendum within 48 hours. Price it below your usual rate as a test. Track close rate.
Why this works: most agency churn is not about performance. It is about perceived attention. When a client feels like a line item on a P&L, they start shopping. When they feel like a partner who gets proactive attention, they renew. The audit costs nothing but time and can surface significant incremental revenue before a single new business pitch is made.
